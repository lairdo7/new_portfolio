Quick explanation

What it is

  • SUBC builds human-powered submarines to race internationally — a diver inside pedals the sub through a course.
  • I work on the drivetrain: a 2:1 straight-bevel gearbox that turns pedalling into propeller rotation, designed for a 1.8–2.1 kN load.
  • FEA-guided material removal cut the side plates from 1.89 lb to 1.19 lb (37%), and a modified step-down collar stopped the driving pinion from slipping.
  • Modelled in SolidWorks, validated with FEA, and CNC machined in-house.

Deep dive

How the drivetrain came together

Part 1: the problem

1. Two things were wrong with the old gearbox

  • It was heavier than it needed to be. The whole vehicle is buoyancy-balanced, so every gram of structure is a gram that has to be offset somewhere else.
  • The driving pinion slipped on its shaft and drifted out of alignment — losing power at exactly the moment the pilot was pushing hardest.
  • Both problems lived in the same two components: the side plates that carry the bearing bores, and the collar that locates the driving pinion.

Part 2: concept & CAD

2. Generating and narrowing the side plate concepts

  • Generated several side plate concepts and scored them against weight, stiffness, and — critically — whether a machinist could actually fixture and cut them.
  • Modelled the shortlist in SolidWorks, iterating the geometry around the bearing bores and mounting interfaces where the loads concentrate.
  • Kept the bearing seats as the fixed reference and let everything else move, so alignment stayed correct through every revision.

Part 3: FEA optimization

3. Letting the analysis remove the material

  • Ran FEA on each iteration against the 1.8–2.1 kN design load and removed material from the low-stress regions the analysis highlighted.
  • Held the required safety factors as the hard constraint, and got the side plates from 1.89 lb down to 1.19 lb — 37% lighter.
  • Several concepts I liked visually didn't hold the safety factor and had to be thrown out. This was the first project where FEA changed my design rather than just confirming it.

Part 4: fixing the slippage

4. Turning a stock collar into a step-down collar

  • The driving pinion was slipping on its shaft and losing alignment, and nothing off the shelf fit the jump from 0.500 in to 0.375 in.
  • Modified a stock McMaster-Carr shaft collar into a 0.500 to 0.375 in step-down collar in SolidWorks, sized for our gearbox and for what we could machine in-house.
  • Made drawings so the fits were unambiguous to whoever cut the part.

Part 5: manufacturing & results

5. Cutting it and proving it

  • Prepared drawings for CNC machining, then installed the finished side plates into the assembly.
  • 37% lighter side plates (1.89 → 1.19 lb) while holding the required safety factors.
  • Pinion slippage eliminated and alignment restored with the step-down collar.

The biggest lesson wasn't the analysis — it was how much manufacturing constrains design. Designing around what we could machine in-house changed my geometry more than any simulation did, because a part you can't fixture is a part you can't make.
