Quick explanation

What it is

  • A 5-DOF robotic arm I took from a first sketch on paper through to a working machine — every part designed in SolidWorks and printed on my Bambu A1.
  • An app in my headset puts a 3D model of the arm in the room in front of me. I drag the end of that model around and the real arm follows it.
  • There's a toggle between a free mode and one that keeps the gripper parallel to the ground.
  • The joints carry their loads on bearings rather than servo output shafts, and the shoulder uses two mirrored servos so they share the torque at the heaviest joint.
  • The gripper is a linear parallel design, so the jaws stay flat to each other and the grip point doesn't move as they open.

Deep dive

How the arm came together

Part 1: why I built it

1. A project I owned end to end

  • I wanted a robotics project that was mine from start to finish — first sketch on paper through to a physical product sitting on the desk.
  • I had a new 3D printer and wanted to learn it properly, rather than only printing other people's files.
  • I also wanted to find out whether what I knew in CAD would hold up on parts I actually had to manufacture myself.

Part 2: driving it from a headset

2. Dragging a model and watching the arm follow

  • An app in the headset opens a 3D model of the arm in AR, floating in the space in front of me.
  • I drag the end of that model wherever I want it and the real arm follows in real time.
  • A toggle switches between a free mode and one that holds the gripper parallel to the ground, which is what you want when you're picking something up off a table.
  • The demo video is filmed through the headset. The AR overlay doesn't show up in the capture, so you see the arm moving but not the model I'm dragging.

Part 3: mechanical design

3. Keeping loads off the servo shafts

  • The shoulder pivot uses two mirrored servos so they share the torque at the joint carrying the greatest load.
  • Each of the two elbows pairs one servo with one bearing on the opposite side, so the radial and bending loads run through the bearing.
  • A servo output shaft is meant to transmit torque, not hold up a moving arm. Putting the bearing opposite it is what keeps those loads off the servo.
  • Every structural part was modelled in SolidWorks and printed in FDM, with off-the-shelf bearings and fasteners.

Part 4: the gripper

4. Why the jaws stay parallel

  • The gripper is a linear parallel design. I worked it up from reference designs I found online, then iterated until it suited my printed tolerances and dropped cleanly into the rest of the arm.
  • Parallel jaws stay flat to each other and travel in a straight line, so the contact point sits in the same place no matter how far the gripper is opened.
  • The fingertips on a pivoting claw swing through an arc as they close, which shifts the grip point and means the arm has to be placed differently for every object width.

Part 5: iteration

5. Three rounds before it went together properly

  • This is the part I learned the most from. Certain parts took roughly three rounds of iteration before the arm assembled the way it was supposed to.
  • Print orientation: working out how to lay a part out so the layer lines aren't facing the load.
  • Tolerances: designing for press fits and for the clearances a printed part actually comes out at.
  • Assembly: no fasteners in places you can't reach with a hand tool. It sounds obvious until you've printed the part that has one.
  • Power: running a system with real current draw rather than something that just blinks an LED.
  • The lesson was that nothing is done in one try, and it shouldn't be. You learn, iterate and improve as you go.

Part 6: the control stack

6. From headset to servo

  • The VR app solves the inverse kinematics and sends joint angles at 50 Hz, the same rate the servos take their PWM signal at.
  • A Node.js bridge on the computer maps those joint angles into servo commands — the headset's browser can't talk to the hardware directly.
  • C++ firmware on an ESP32 handles the servo communication and enforces velocity and acceleration limits so nothing snaps to a new position.
  • It runs over serial when the board is plugged into the computer, or UDP over Wi-Fi when it isn't.

Part 7: what's next

7. Replacing the nest of jumper wires

  • The next thing I want to do is design a PCB to replace the breadboard.
  • That puts the servo power distribution, the ESP32 and the signal routing onto a single board instead of a nest of jumper wires.
  • The wiring is easily the least reliable part of the project right now, so it's the obvious thing to fix next.
